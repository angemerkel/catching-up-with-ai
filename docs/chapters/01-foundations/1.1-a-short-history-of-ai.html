1.1 A short history of AI

Published

16 December 2025

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been around for more than 70 years, but the release of ChatGPT in 2022 brought it to the attention of a much wider audience. What has changed is not only what computers can do, but also how researchers approach the problem of making machines perform tasks that we associate with intelligence.

When I started exploring generative AI, I wanted to understand more about how we got here. The following is a brief overview of the major developments and milestones, along with the companies and models that have shaped today’s AI landscape.

1950s–1980s: The foundations

Early AI research focused on whether machines could imitate human intelligence, primarily through symbolic reasoning and rule-based systems. Researchers also began exploring artificial neural networks and methods for learning from data.

  • 1950 – Alan Turing publishes Computing Machinery and Intelligence, proposing what later became known as the Turing Test as a way to examine machine intelligence (Turing 1950).

  • 1956 – The term artificial intelligence is introduced in the proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project, organised by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon. The workshop is generally considered a founding event of AI research.

  • 1960s–1970s – Early conversational systems such as ELIZA (1966, Joseph Weizenbaum, MIT) simulate conversation using pattern matching and predefined rules.

  • 1970s–1980s – Expert systems, such as MYCIN at Stanford, use explicitly encoded knowledge and rules to support decision-making in specialised domains.

  • 1986 – Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularise backpropagation for training multilayer neural networks, laying important groundwork for modern deep learning (Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams 1986).

1990s–2000s: Statistical machine learning and neural networks

Statistical machine learning became increasingly important, while advances in neural-network architectures provided new approaches to image recognition and sequential data. However, progress in deep learning remained constrained by computing power and the availability of training data.

  • 1990s–2000s – Statistical methods, including support vector machines, decision trees and ensemble methods, become widely used for classification, prediction and pattern recognition.

  • 1997 – Hochreiter and Schmidhuber introduce Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, designed to capture long-range dependencies in sequential data (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997).

  • 1998 – Yann LeCun and colleagues demonstrate convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for handwritten character recognition with LeNet-5 (LeCun et al. 1998).

  • 2006 – Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues demonstrate methods for training deep neural networks, contributing to renewed interest in deep learning (Hinton, Osindero, and Teh 2006).

2010s: Deep learning and the emergence of generative AI

Larger datasets, improved algorithms and powerful graphics processing units (GPUs) enabled major advances in deep learning, particularly in image recognition, speech processing and natural language processing. New architectures also expanded the possibilities for generating content.

  • 2012 – Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton achieve a major improvement in the ImageNet image classification competition with AlexNet, demonstrating the potential of deep CNNs trained using GPUs (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton 2012).

  • 2014 – Ian Goodfellow and colleagues introduce Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which train two competing neural networks to generate realistic data (Goodfellow et al. 2014).

  • 2014 – Kingma and Welling introduce Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), another influential approach to deep generative modelling (Kingma and Welling 2014).

  • 2015 – OpenAI is founded as an AI research organisation.

  • 2017 – Vaswani and colleagues introduce the Transformer architecture in Attention Is All You Need. Based on attention mechanisms, transformers enable more parallelised training than earlier recurrent approaches and become central to modern large language models (Vaswani et al. 2017).

2018–2020: The beginning of the large language model era

Transformer-based architectures made it possible to train models on increasingly large text datasets. Pretraining on general-purpose data, followed by adaptation to specific tasks, became a central approach in natural language processing.

  • 2018 – Google introduces BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), demonstrating the effectiveness of bidirectional pretraining for language understanding.

  • 2018 – OpenAI introduces GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer), combining transformer-based language modelling with generative pretraining.

  • 2018–2019 – Hugging Face develops its open-source Transformers library, making pretrained language models easier to access and reuse. Together with the Hugging Face Hub, it helps establish an open ecosystem for sharing models and datasets (Wolf et al. 2020).

  • 2019 – OpenAI releases GPT-2, demonstrating improved text generation. Google introduces T5, which treats different natural language processing tasks as text-to-text problems.

  • 2020 – OpenAI introduces GPT-3, a language model with 175 billion parameters, demonstrating improved few-shot learning across a range of language tasks (Brown et al. 2020).

  • 2020 – Research on denoising diffusion probabilistic models provides an important foundation for later image-generation systems (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020).

2021–2023: Foundation models and generative AI become widely accessible

Large pretrained models increasingly serve as the basis for applications across different tasks. Meanwhile, conversational interfaces and image-generation tools bring generative AI to a much wider audience.

  • 2021 – The concept of foundation models gains prominence, describing models trained on broad datasets that can be adapted to different tasks (Bommasani et al. 2021). Anthropic is founded, with a focus on AI safety, alignment and interpretability.

  • 2022 – OpenAI releases DALL·E 2, while Stable Diffusion makes text-to-image generation more widely accessible.

  • November 2022 – OpenAI launches ChatGPT, bringing conversational interfaces based on large language models to a broad public audience.

  • 2023 – OpenAI releases GPT-4, expanding the capabilities of the GPT family, including support for image input.

  • 2023 – Anthropic introduces Claude, Meta releases Llama and Llama 2, and Google DeepMind introduces Gemini, expanding the range of competing language models.

  • 2023 – Generative AI becomes increasingly integrated into software development and productivity tools, including GitHub Copilot and Microsoft Copilot.

2024–2025: Multimodal AI, reasoning models and AI assistants

Generative AI moves beyond text-based chatbots. Models increasingly support multiple types of input and output, while advances in reasoning and tool integration expand what AI assistants can do.

  • 2024 – OpenAI introduces GPT-4o, supporting real-time interaction with text, images and audio. Google’s Gemini 1.5 and Anthropic’s Claude 3 demonstrate further advances in multimodal capabilities and long-context processing.

  • 2024 – OpenAI introduces the o1 model family, using additional computation during inference to improve performance on reasoning tasks.

  • December 2024 – DeepSeek-V3 demonstrates competitive performance using a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture and techniques to improve training and inference efficiency (DeepSeek-AI 2024).

  • January 2025 – DeepSeek-R1 attracts attention for its reasoning capabilities and use of reinforcement learning, contributing to the growing ecosystem of open-weight models (DeepSeek-AI 2025).

  • 2025 – AI coding assistants, including GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code and OpenAI Codex, increasingly support software development beyond code completion.

  • 2025 – AI systems increasingly combine language models with external tools, information retrieval and multi-step workflows, driving interest in AI agents and more autonomous assistants.

Major companies, models and tools

Generative AI has created a diverse landscape of research organisations, model developers, technology providers and user-facing applications. Some companies develop their own foundation models, while others build applications or infrastructure around existing models.

The following table provides a selection of major players and their contributions as of December 2025.

Company / organisation Key contributions Notable models / tools
OpenAI Large language models, image generation, reasoning models GPT series, o-series, DALL·E, ChatGPT, Codex
Google / Google DeepMind Transformer research, language and multimodal models BERT, T5, Gemini, Imagen, Gemini app
Anthropic Language models, AI safety and alignment research Claude, Claude Code
Meta AI Open-weight language models and AI research Llama family, Meta AI
DeepSeek Efficient language models, reasoning models and open-weight releases DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek Chat
Mistral AI Open-weight and commercial language models Mistral, Mixtral, Le Chat
Hugging Face Open-source ML libraries, model and dataset sharing, collaborative development Transformers, Hugging Face Hub, Datasets, Spaces
Microsoft / GitHub Integration of AI into productivity software, development tools and cloud services Microsoft Copilot, GitHub Copilot, Azure AI
Stability AI Generative image models and open-weight releases Stable Diffusion
Midjourney / Runway Image and video generation Midjourney, Runway video-generation models
NVIDIA Hardware and software infrastructure for AI training and inference GPUs, CUDA, DGX systems

This is a selective overview rather than a comprehensive comparison. The table includes models, applications and underlying technologies; some organisations operate across several categories.

Further reading

For a more comprehensive review of the history and development of generative AI:

  • He, R., Cao, J. & Tan, T. (2025). Generative artificial intelligence: a historical perspective. National Science Review, 12(5), nwaf050. Reviews the development of generative AI over seven decades, from rule-based systems to foundation models.

  • Sengar, S. S., Hasan, A. B., Kumar, S. & Carroll, F. (2025; published online 2024). Generative artificial intelligence: a systematic review and applications. Multimedia Tools and Applications, 84, 23661–23700. Reviews generative AI techniques and applications, particularly developments between 2018 and 2023.
